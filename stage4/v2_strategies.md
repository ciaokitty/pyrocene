# Strategies: five additions around the vulnerability window

Brainstorm of 20 September 2026. Follows `v1_policy_gaming.md` and reads
`.prompt/stage4_addons.md` (the Ledger and Vigilance experiments) as evidence
to choose from, not as a specification. Every name and price here is a
proposal, not a current rule. Nothing in this file is built.

The vulnerability window should be the centre, and the other four additions
target it.

## Framing, corrected 21 September

The goal is **durable forest**. Reduced wildfire is one of several good
consequences of it, not the objective. Fuel connectivity is one rule inside the
game, not its thesis; `narrative/GAME_DESIGN.md` §2 currently says otherwise and
is being revised.

Climate change is the **difficulty, not the villain**. It never appears on a
card. Naming it would make a caption of a mechanic, and it would put the one
thing the player cannot change into the slot marked beat this. Where it lives is
the composition of the season deck in §5 below: how often the dry season comes,
and how much goes wrong inside the window. Later missions deal more dry seasons.
The deck is face up. Nobody says why; the facilitator says why, if asked.

There is also **no boss**. The end of the arc is a handover: the season where
nobody works a patch and it is still there. A forest you have to keep saving is
not restored.

## 1. Make the vulnerability window the thing on screen

- **What the player sees:** each planted patch shows a countdown to closure, as
  a ring on its pin, for example "three seasons to close".
- **The ledger:** it is the set of open windows.
- **After closure:** a closed canopy is safe, as a stated simplification.
  Nothing outside a window needs the crew.

This also suggests a fourth payment rule for the funder ending: pay on closure.
The funder verifies once that the canopy closed and pays a lump sum then. That
could bridge the gap that bankrupts mission 2.

## 2. Three stressors that strike only inside the window, each with a matching answer

- **Grass** smothers saplings.
- **Fire** burns them.
- **A quiet stressor** stalls or kills them with nothing announced.

Give the player one purchase with three settings, a care contract. It pays
people to weed, to watch for fire, or to treat the quiet cause, with one setting
per patch. A wrong setting costs money and does nothing.

The Vigilance lab's cheap agents made this mistake unprompted. One bought a
firebreak while grass was the damage. Another kept tending while dry soil was
holding growth back. That says the diagnosis is a real decision.

The contract should run for the whole window and appear in the "each season"
income line on the HUD. That avoids the hidden expiry trap the lab found with
renewals.

## 3. Before and after in the structure panel

The structure panel would show last season on the left and now on the right. It
is free and costs attention, not credits. Paid sensors were the weakest part of
the Vigilance lab, and this replaces them.

Each cause leaves a signature in the point cloud:

- **Grass:** pink low growth rising among the stems.
- **Fire:** a brown ground layer with the canopy still standing.
- **Drought:** saplings present but no taller, with a few gaps and no pink.
- **Browsing:** tops missing at one height, stems intact, and a worn line in
  from one edge.
- **Lianas:** a tangle over the saplings. The structure lab already models
  climbers.

The game would describe only what is observed, in field language, and never
name the cause. "Tops missing at a metre, stems intact, a worn line from the
east" is something a group can search for, put to an outside AI, or ask the
person next to them.

This also gives the ecologist team a job after round one. They diagnose the
cause, and the removal team funds the answer.

## 4. The community as carers, not as the problem

The care contract is paid local stewardship. The disturbances that come from
the community are livelihoods running into the window: cattle need grazing, and
pasture gets burned to renew it.

One instrument, a village agreement bought once per mission. It is either a
grazing corridor or an early cool burn of the pasture edge, carried out by the
community. The early burn defuses the dry-season fire.

This is also the prescribed burn's actual niche. The lab parked the burn because
paid clearance always beat it as a way to clear a patch. As fire prevention at
the source, it has a purpose.

Separately, the player can ask the village once per mission for a hint in plain
words, as `ask` works in the online game.

## 5. Named strategies and a season deck

**Named strategies.** Offer three or four, each with one line and a stated
weakness:

- **Rush:** fast if nothing goes wrong. The lab measured it succeeding in 2 of
  100 gentle landscapes and 0 of 100 severe ones.
- **One at a time:** safe and slow.
- **Anchor and spread:** plant beside forest so the patches shelter each other.
- **Mine and hold:** work two patches for income and fund one window fully.

In a room, deal each team a different strategy to argue for. The end card then
names the strategy they actually played.

**Season deck.** This is the randomness, and it is also where climate lives.
Each season one card is drawn: dry year, wet year, stray cattle, or a quiet
season.

- Clear events are telegraphed one season ahead. Quiet events are not.
- Every team plays the same seed.
- Whether a fire starts becomes a probability tied to the year. Spread stays
  decided by connected fuel.
- Fire is then one stressor among several. At present it arrives every third
  season regardless.

**The deck is face up.** Show the remaining cards' backs and the decade's
composition, for example "12 seasons, 3 dry". The player can then plan, which is
the point. The composition shifts across missions: one dry season in six, then
two, then three. That shift is the only statement the game makes about a
changing climate, and it is a count rather than a claim.

## 6. What a durable forest gives back

If durability is the goal, the game currently under-rewards it. Forest health is
one abstract number. The payoff should arrive as several named things, so the
player can see what they bought:

- **Fire stays out**, because it is damp and shaded. Modelled.
- **Animals return** and carry canopy seed, so the next stand enriches itself
  free. Modelled.
- **It shelters the next plot**, making neighbouring work cheaper. Modelled via
  hostile neighbours.
- **Carbon pays, and keeps paying.** Modelled.
- **The creek runs later** into the dry season. Not modelled.
- **Shade-grown produce.** Income that does not require cutting anything. Not
  modelled.

The last one matters most. At present the community's only income is clearing,
so the forest is worth something only while a funder is writing cheques. Give
mature forest a livelihood and the community has its own reason to want it. That
closes the socio-economic loop the rest of this file opens.

Both unmodelled items need sourcing before they carry a number. Dry-season
streamflow after tropical restoration is contested in the literature and should
not be asserted as a simple gain.

## Leave out

Paid surveys, seed monitoring, multiple protection types and soil gadgets. One
contract, one agreement and one free instrument should be enough.

## Smallest build that could disprove this

Add only browsing, the three-setting contract, and the before and after panel.
Then run three scripted crews:

- one that diagnoses the cause
- one that always picks weeding
- one that buys everything

If the three crews end up about the same, the idea is wrong.

## The question that needs real people

Can ten people work out the cause from the before and after panel in about two
minutes, and do they enjoy arguing about it?

## Open

Should the quiet stressor in the guided mode be browsing, which ties into the
community angle, or drought, which ties into the dry-year card?

Browsing, lianas, community burn calendars and grazing agreements are named here
from general knowledge. Source them before any of it carries a real place name
or a species.
